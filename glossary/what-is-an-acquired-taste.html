What is an acquired taste, and why do tastes change?

By the Taste10 team · Updated November 2026

An acquired taste is a food or drink that most people dislike at first and learn to like only after trying it several times, such as black coffee, olives, blue cheese or very dark chocolate. Repeated tasting makes the unfamiliar feel safe and familiar, and many people end up loving what they once rejected.

Why some foods are an acquired taste

  1. Bitterness: people are wary of bitter tastes from the start, so coffee, dark chocolate and bitter greens often need time.
  2. Strong smells: blue cheese, fermented fish and durian can smell like something has gone off.
  3. Unusual textures: oysters, natto or okra feel strange before they taste good.
  4. Unfamiliarity: anything far from the food we grew up with needs a few tries.

Why tasting again works

Liking grows with exposure. In a classic study, young children were offered unfamiliar foods again and again, and the more often they had tasted a food, the more they liked it (Birch and Marlin, 1982). Adults work the same way: each try makes a food more familiar, and familiar food tastes better to us.

Why tastes change over a lifetime

Children tend to prefer sweet and avoid bitter. As we grow up, try more foods and eat in new places, many of us come to enjoy flavours we once avoided. Habits, culture and the people we eat with all shape it, which is why your taste today may be quite different from your taste ten years ago.

How to acquire a taste

  1. Start small, with a little alongside food you already like.
  2. Try a milder version first: a milky coffee before an espresso, a creamy blue cheese before a sharp one.
  3. Try it more than once. A single bad first impression says little.

Questions and answers

What is an example of an acquired taste?

Black coffee, olives, blue cheese, very dark chocolate and anchovies are classic examples: many people dislike them at first and like them later.

How many times do you need to try a food to like it?

There is no fixed number, but research with children suggests that liking often grows after repeated tasting, sometimes ten or more times.

Why do tastes change as we get older?

Repeated exposure, new experiences, culture and habit all shape what we enjoy, so many people come to like flavours they avoided as children.
